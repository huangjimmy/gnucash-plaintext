A BORROWED DOLLAR, WORKED THROUGH
=================================

A Canadian book borrows 1,000.00 USD at 1% for a year, never spends it, is paid
1,010.00 USD by a customer in the meantime, and repays 1,010.00 USD at the end.
Every cost basis it opens and consumes is written out below, with the
transaction that does it.

Why this case: it is the smallest book that touches both sides of the cost
bases. A borrowing raises the owed side and the held side at once, interest
raises the owed side on its own, and the repayment lowers both — so it says
what a cost basis has to do at every point, and it is the case that showed the
refusal in `refuse_a_disposal_that_gives_no_cost_basis` had to ask per side
rather than per currency.

The rates, which are the whole of the arithmetic:

    2026-01-02   1.30 CAD per USD    the loan is drawn
    2026-06-30   1.35 CAD per USD    the customer pays
    2027-01-02   1.40 CAD per USD    the loan is repaid

Read `cost_basis_balance:` as "this split brought in this many units and this
many are still unspent", and `cost_basis_split_guid:` as "these units came out
of that split". The guids below are written in words, as <the 1,000.00
borrowing basis>, so the working can be followed; a real file writes 32 hex
characters. It has two ways to get them. `fx-balances` prints the guid of every
cost basis a book already holds, which is what a file written after the fact
uses. A file that opens a cost basis and consumes it in one run cannot do that,
because GnuCash assigns the guid as the file is read — so it writes `guid:` on
the split itself, which the import obeys, and gives that same guid to the
disposal below. `tests/fixtures/account_balance_test_data.txt` is the shortest
example: one opening purchase of 8,000.00 HKD carrying its own guid, and one
fare consuming 300.00 of it.


t0 — THE LOAN IS DRAWN
----------------------

    2026-01-02 * "Borrow 1,000.00 USD for a year at 1%"
    	currency.mnemonic: "CAD"
    	Assets:USD Bank 1000.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.30000"
    		value: "1300.00"
    		cost_basis_balance: "1000.00"
    	Liabilities:USD Loan -1000.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.30000"
    		value: "-1300.00"
    		cost_basis_balance: "1000.00"

**Two cost bases, one on each side, and both are right.** The book now holds
1,000.00 USD that cost it 1,300.00 CAD, and owes 1,000.00 USD that it received
1,300.00 CAD for. Those are different facts about different obligations and
neither stands in for the other.

    asset      1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Assets:USD Bank
    liability  1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Liabilities:USD Loan

    bank holds 1,000.00 USD          agrees with the asset basis
    loan owes  1,000.00 USD          agrees with the liability basis

The book is flat in US dollars: it holds as many as it owes. If the dollar
moves tomorrow, the gain on the money it holds is the loss on the money it owes
and the two cancel — which is what a borrowing nobody has spent should do, and
only shows up as nil because both sides are measured.


t1 — THE CUSTOMER PAYS
----------------------

    2026-06-30 * "Customer pays invoice INV-1, 1,010.00 USD"
    	currency.mnemonic: "CAD"
    	Assets:USD Bank 1010.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.35000"
    		value: "1363.50"
    		cost_basis_balance: "1010.00"
    	Income:Consulting -1363.50 CAD

A third cost basis, on the held side, at a different rate from the first. Two
bases of one currency on one account, and they stay two: 1,010.00 USD taken on
at 1.35 is not 1,000.00 taken on at 1.30, and a disposal will have to say which
of them it draws on.

    asset      1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Assets:USD Bank
    asset      1,010.00 USD  at 1.35 CAD  =  1,363.50 CAD   Assets:USD Bank
    liability  1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Liabilities:USD Loan

    bank holds 2,010.00 USD          agrees with the asset bases
    loan owes  1,000.00 USD          agrees with the liability basis


t2 — THE INTEREST IS OWED
-------------------------

    2027-01-02 * "Interest for the year, 10.00 USD"
    	currency.mnemonic: "CAD"
    	Expenses:Interest 14.00 CAD
    	Liabilities:USD Loan -10.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.40000"
    		value: "-14.00"
    		cost_basis_balance: "10.00"

**Interest raises the owed side, so it opens a cost basis like any other
arrival.** The book now owes 10.00 USD it did not owe before, and what that
obligation cost is 14.00 CAD — the expense recognised the day it arose. Nothing
about it differs from the borrowing except the size and the rate.

    asset      1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Assets:USD Bank
    asset      1,010.00 USD  at 1.35 CAD  =  1,363.50 CAD   Assets:USD Bank
    liability  1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Liabilities:USD Loan
    liability     10.00 USD  at 1.40 CAD  =     14.00 CAD   Liabilities:USD Loan

    bank holds 2,010.00 USD          agrees
    loan owes  1,010.00 USD          agrees


t3 — THE LOAN IS REPAID
-----------------------

    2027-01-02 * "Repay the loan, 1,010.00 USD"
    	currency.mnemonic: "CAD"
    	Liabilities:USD Loan 1000.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.30000"
    		value: "1300.00"
    		cost_basis_split_guid: "<the 1,000.00 borrowing basis>"
    	Liabilities:USD Loan 10.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.40000"
    		value: "14.00"
    		cost_basis_split_guid: "<the 10.00 interest basis>"
    	Assets:USD Bank -1010.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.35000"
    		value: "-1363.50"
    		cost_basis_split_guid: "<the 1,010.00 invoice basis>"
    	Expenses:Foreign exchange loss $residual$ CAD

**Every split leaves at what its units cost, not at today's rate**: 1.30 on the
principal, 1.40 on the interest, 1.35 on the dollars the customer paid. That is
the convention every disposal in this format follows, and it is what leaves
something for `$residual$` to take:

    1,300.00 + 14.00 - 1,363.50 = -49.50

so the residual is 49.50 CAD of loss. Written at 1.40 throughout, the
transaction balances by itself and the import refuses it — `$residual$ has
nothing to take — the other splits already balance` — which is the right
refusal, because a disposal whose splits balance realized nothing, and this one
realized 49.50.

**Nothing states `took_the_residual` beside `$residual$`.** The two are the two
spellings of the same thing: write the token and the import works the figure
out and writes the key on the split it resolved to, or work the figure out by
hand, write it, and state the key yourself. Writing both says once what the
import is about to say anyway.

**Three guids, because three cost bases are consumed**, and the loan split is
written twice, once per basis it draws on. This is the transaction
that decides the shape of the refusal. The dollars leave the bank — an asset
basis is consumed — and the debt they pay off goes — a liability basis is
consumed. They were taken on at different rates and on different days, so each
realizes a difference of its own, and one guid cannot say both.

    asset side
      consumed  1,010.00 USD at 1.35  =  1,363.50 CAD of cost
      fetched   1,010.00 USD at 1.40  =  1,414.00 CAD
      realized                          +   50.50 CAD    a gain

    liability side
      consumed  1,000.00 USD at 1.30  =  1,300.00 CAD of cost
      paid off  1,000.00 USD at 1.40  =  1,400.00 CAD
      realized                          -  100.00 CAD    a loss
      consumed     10.00 USD at 1.40  =     14.00 CAD of cost
      paid off     10.00 USD at 1.40  =     14.00 CAD
      realized                                0.00 CAD

Note the liability side needs both of its bases, 1,000.00 and 10.00, to make up
the 1,010.00 paid off. Which of them a repayment draws on is the file's to say
exactly as it is on the held side; the two above happen to be taken in full, so
the file could give either guid first, but a partial repayment could not.

    asset      1,000.00 USD  at 1.30 CAD  =  1,300.00 CAD   Assets:USD Bank
    liability      0.00 USD                                 both spent to nothing

    bank holds 1,000.00 USD          agrees with the asset basis
    loan owes      0.00 USD          agrees: nothing owed, nothing left based

A cost basis spent to nothing stays on the book reading 0.00 rather than
disappearing — `fx-balances` lists it and `--with-balance-only` is the flag for
hiding it — so what is checked here is the balance, never the absence of a row.


t4 — THE DOLLARS THE LOAN BROUGHT IN ARE SOLD
---------------------------------------------

    2027-01-03 * "Sell the remaining 1,000.00 USD at 1.40"
    	currency.mnemonic: "CAD"
    	Assets:Bank 1400.00 CAD
    	Assets:USD Bank -1000.00 USD
    		account.commodity.mnemonic: "USD"
    		share_price: "1.30000"
    		value: "-1300.00"
    		cost_basis_split_guid: "<the 1,000.00 borrowing basis, held side>"
    	Income:FX Gain $residual$ CAD

They cost 1.30 and fetch 1.40, so they leave at 1,300.00 against 1,400.00 taken
in and the residual is a gain of 100.00 CAD — exactly what the owed side lost
on the same dollars at t3.

**An exchange gain account, not a revenue one.** What this realizes is a gain
on having held US dollars, and booking it to `Income:Consulting` would put it
on the income statement as money earned from customers, inside `total_revenue`,
where nothing on the page would say otherwise.

    every US dollar figure is now nought:

    asset bases      0.00 USD        bank holds  0.00 USD
    liability bases  0.00 USD        loan owes   0.00 USD


WHAT THE BOOK MADE, CHECKED TWO WAYS
------------------------------------

From the bases:

    +  50.50 CAD   gain on the dollars the customer paid, held 1.35 -> 1.40
    - 100.00 CAD   loss on the principal, owed 1.30 -> 1.40
       0.00 CAD   on the interest, owed and paid at the same rate
    ------------
    -  49.50 CAD   realized on foreign currency

    and separately, 14.00 CAD of interest expense.

From the cash, which must agree:

    borrowed  1,000.00 USD at 1.30  =  1,300.00 CAD of value received
    repaid    1,010.00 USD at 1.40  =  1,414.00 CAD of value given up
    ------------
    the borrowing cost                   114.00 CAD

    of which 14.00 CAD is interest — 10.00 USD at 1.40 — and the remaining
    100.00 CAD is the currency moving against a debt held in it.

    the dollars the customer paid rose 1.35 -> 1.40 on 1,010.00
                                       =  +50.50 CAD

    114.00 - 14.00 = 100.00 loss on the loan, against 50.50 gained on the
    receipts: -49.50 CAD realized, and 14.00 CAD of interest. The two ways
    agree.

**The two exposures are real and opposite, and only a book that measures both
sides finds them.** The loan is a short position in US dollars for a year and
the receipt is a long one for six months. Netting the sides together — treating
the repayment as though it moved nothing because the splits sum to zero in US
dollars — hides a 100.00 CAD loss behind a 50.50 CAD gain and reports -49.50 as
though nothing had happened twice.


WHAT THIS CASE SETTLED
----------------------

1. **A borrowing opens two cost bases**, one per side. Already the behaviour,
   and this shows why: the held dollars and the owed dollars are separate
   exposures taken on at the same rate and disposed of at different times.

2. **Interest opens a liability cost basis.** It raises the owed side, and
   every arrival on a side is costed.

3. **A repayment consumes a basis on each side, so it gives a guid on each
   side.** `refuse_a_disposal_that_gives_no_cost_basis` asked per currency
   first, which let a repayment giving only the loan's guid through while the
   bank's dollars went unaccounted for — probe case 4 exactly: the liability
   agreed, the asset was 2,000.00 adrift, exit 0. It asks per side now.

4. **A currency's bases are consumed by guid, never by rule.** At t3 the held
   side has two bases at 1.30 and 1.35, and which one the repayment draws on
   moves the realized figure by 50.50 CAD. Nothing in the book prefers one, so
   nothing may choose: the file says, or the import refuses.

5. **Every split of a disposal leaves at what its units cost.** Written at the
   day's rate instead, the transaction balances by itself and `$residual$` is
   refused for having nothing to take. That refusal is right, and it is how the
   format keeps a disposal from silently realizing nothing.

6. **A borrowing nobody spends ends level, and only both sides show it.** The
   100.00 CAD lost on owing the dollars is the 100.00 gained on holding them.
   Measured on one side alone the book appears to have lost 100.00 on a loan it
   never used.

This whole walkthrough is a test:
`tests/integration/test_a_borrowed_dollar_keeps_its_cost_bases_in_step.py` runs
these five transactions one at a time and asks the invariant before and after
each of them, ending on every US dollar figure being nought.
